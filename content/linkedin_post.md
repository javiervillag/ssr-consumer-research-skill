# LinkedIn Post — SSR Consumer Research (213 words)

A concept test costs $20,000 and takes six weeks. A new research paper just got 90% of the way there for pennies.

Here's the trick.

Ask an LLM to rate a product 1 to 5 and it says 3. Every time. Useless.

So researchers stopped asking for numbers. They asked for an opinion, the way a real customer would talk. Then they compared that opinion to reference phrases — "I'd definitely buy this" or "I'm not interested" — and scored it based on how similar it sounded.

The text becomes the rating. They call it Semantic Similarity Rating (SSR).

Tested against 9,300 real human responses across 57 personal care product surveys, it hit roughly 90% of human test-retest reliability. The synthetic panel agreed with itself almost as consistently as humans do.

Now, the honest part. The viral tweet about this paper earned a community note, and fairly. The method is only validated on personal care products, it doesn't reliably replicate gender or regional subgroups, and it is not a replacement for human panels.

What it is: a dirt-cheap first pass. Screen ten concepts with SSR, then spend your real research budget on the two survivors.

Test more. Waste less. That's the whole play.
